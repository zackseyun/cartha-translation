## Interpretation and decision

Himbaza argues that ethnos preceded laos here and links the change to later differentiation of Israel from other nations. He also warns against creating an eclectic Greek text by inserting isolated earlier terms into another version's style. This is his argument, not an established rule that ethnos cannot denote Israel. [Himbaza, pp110–115](https://www.peeters-leuven.be/pdf/9789042943452.pdf).

Wevers' 2005 discussion does not establish the opposite priority: he finds the Leviticus usage inconclusive and leaves Rahlfs unchanged. Crucially, he says both Greek nouns can represent Hebrew עם. His collation also distinguishes dative μοι from genitive μου; this is not merely a spelling difference. [Wevers, pp2–4](https://septuaginta.uni-goettingen.de/static/ioscs/pdf/volumes/bioscs38.pdf).

Our inference is therefore narrower than either a restoration claim or blanket preference for the traditional Greek: the selected evidence does **not require a different Hebrew Vorlage**. Retroverting גוי because a Greek edition reads ethnos would be unjustified here. A translation explicitly representing this Greek witness could expose “nation/people” and the dative construction in an apparatus; that does not compel replacement of Hebrew-based POB's “my people.”

The current POB and pinned Greek were read in 26:11–13 context: divine presence, the reciprocal God/people relationship and deliverance from Egypt. This does not settle the separate dwelling/covenant difference in v11 or opening-clause issue in v12. The selected English preserves the covenant relation; “a people to me” is a more formal alternative, not a demonstrated semantic restoration.
